## Persona doc

**Who he actually is**

A technical founder who is pre-product-market-fit and knows it - his entire worldview right now is "solve the right problem before the money runs out." He came from a large company (Stripe), left to build something he wanted to use himself, which means he has unusually high standards for product quality and unusually low patience for tools that don't work exactly as advertised. At 9 people, he is probably doing 4 jobs simultaneously.

**Communication style**

Extremely terse. His About section is 4 sentences and he explicitly says "short About because I'm busy." His posts are under 80 words each and they almost always end with a specific actionable observation, never a question to drive engagement. He has zero patience for social niceties in outreach. He's told you exactly how to pitch him in his About section ("lead with the problem you solve and your best customer story"); ignoring that instruction is disqualifying.

**What he actually cares about**

Survival: closing customers, not running out of money, making correct bets on which problems to solve. He's not thinking about Q3 roadmaps or org design - he's thinking about this week's revenue. His post about closing 3 customers in 6 weeks with an 11-word template tells you two things: he thinks about process even at this stage, and he respects work that produces results with minimal overhead. He will evaluate your pitch the same way he evaluates everything else: does this move the needle on something that matters?

**Opener patterns that land vs. flop**

Lands: sentence 1 is a problem statement, not a greeting. Includes one specific customer story with real numbers or real outcomes. Asks exactly one question. Total message under 60 words. No opener-warming, no flattery, no "I've been following your journey."

Flops: any opener that starts with praise for Stackform (he posted about this specifically - and they've barely shipped). Long intros explaining your company before explaining the problem. Asking for a 30-minute call in the first message. Anything that requires him to do research to understand what you're offering.

**Likely objections**

- "We're 9 people. Whatever you're selling, I probably can't buy it until we hit product-market fit and have budget."
- "What's the ROI timeline? I don't have runway for 6-month implementations."
- "We use X already and it mostly works. What does this do that X doesn't?"

**Probable buying mode**

Constrained - he'll buy if it solves an acute problem, is cheap or has a clear ROI, and doesn't require him to invest significant time. He's not browsing vendors; he's reacting to specific pain. The only way into a technical founder at seed stage is being directly relevant to a problem they're actively trying to solve this week. If you catch him at the right moment, it closes fast. If you don't, it won't matter how good the pitch is.

## Debrief

**What landed:**

Attempt 3 got a substantive question. The structure was right - problem statement first, one specific customer story with real numbers, specific ask. The customer story format (7-person B2B infra company) was good targeting: you matched his company profile deliberately and he noticed. The $99/mo price transparency removed a common objection before he raised it.

His response was a diagnostic question, not a brush-off. That's the goal with a founder at this stage - you're not trying to book a meeting, you're trying to get a real question. You got one.

**What fell flat:**

Attempt 1 triggered the exact thing he posted about: flattery from someone who hasn't done research. "I love what you're building" when they haven't shipped anything is an immediate tell. He told you this himself in a public post. Reading someone's posts before pitching them is the minimum.

Attempt 2 had the right structure but the technical detail conversation went sideways. The browser extension objection was legitimate and you didn't have a good answer. That's okay - it's real signal. But notice how fast he disqualified once you hit a technical limitation: this is a founder who has been burned by flaky integrations and will not absorb that risk again. If you sell to technical founders, lead with your most reliable integration path, not your broadest feature set.

**One thing to try next time:**

Founders at this stage make buy/no-buy decisions fast because they have to. If they ask a clarifying question, they're basically already saying "this might be relevant." Respond with two sentences max: answer their question, then ask one forward question. Don't re-explain the product. Don't pivot to a demo pitch. Follow the thread they opened. Arjun's question about close rate vs. lead volume was him telling you which metric he cares about - that's the whole next conversation.
